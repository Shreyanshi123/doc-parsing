Novo’s Earnings Story so Far: Meteoric Rise, Unfortunate Fall

Why did Novo deliver a sustained period of earnings upgrades? To recap, Novo was an early player in the GLP-1 market, with Victoza (active ingredient liraglutide) gaining FDA approval in 2010. This was the first once-daily human GLP-1 approved for type 2 diabetes.

1. GLP1 penetration of US diabetes market. Novo launched Ozempic in the US in 2017, a once weekly GLP-1 for type 2 diabetes management. Since then, GLP-1s penetration in the US diabetes market has steadily increased (Exhibit 9). In our view this is primarily because, unlike insulin, these products lead to weight loss rather than weight gain.

2. Inflection of the US obesity market for GLP-1s. The weight-loss benefits enjoyed by patients on Ozempic increased investors’ expectations of the potential for GLP-1s to be used as broader weight management medications - with many patients using Ozempic “off-label” (i.e. for weight-loss and not for diabetes management). Subsequently, data from the phase 3 SELECT outcomes trial in 3Q23 inflected perceptions of the potential of GLP-1s in weight loss. This is because in SELECT Wegovy lowered cardiovascular risk (a composite of reducing heart attacks, strokes, and other CV related deaths) by 20%. This proved that GLP-1s deliver medicinal value beyond weight-loss.

What has caused this downgrade cycle? There have been three major events in our view:

• Poor execution on manufacturing. We think Novo’s strategy for semaglutide production is a prime study for “failure to

prepare is preparation to fail”. Here is a recap of key events:

• Novo let demand for semaglutide products (namely Ozempic and Wegovy) run ahead of their (known) installed capacity

for quality checking and fill-finishing. In March 2022, the FDA announced a shortage of semaglutide, under section 503b. This led to compounding pharmacies entering the US market to ease the semaglutide shortage.

• Novo has invested $25bn since 2022 in capex to bolster fill-finish (putting the active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) into pen injection devices). Then, in early 2025, the FDA announced that the semaglutide shortage was over. However, compounding pharmacies have proven very difficult to remove from the market. Our channel checks indicate are these drugs still command 1/3 of the US market on a volume basis.

WHAT’S THE RISK REWARD?

An Overview of Our Bull-Bear Scenario Analysis

We have constructed a bull-bear scenario approach for Novo sales and EBIT impact across to 2035 (we believe 2035e EBIT is a good DCF proxy). We have focused our bull-bear on the key portfolio products (Ozempic, Wegovy, Oral Sema) and major late stage pipeline assets (CagriSema in obesity and diabetes). In Exhibit 27 we provide a summary table.

Our bull scenario:

• +29% upside to 2035E sales and 43% upside to 2035E EBIT

• Considering the variance of Bernstein vs consensus across portfolio and pipeline assets, we have taken consensus

estimates as our “bull case” for Ozempic, Wegovy, Oral Sema, and CagriSema. In Exhibit 15 and Exhibit 16 we illustrate how our sales estimates compare to consensus for 2035e sales and EBIT respectively.

• In the approved portfolio, we project upsides of 4%, +2%, and +2% to Ozempic, Wegovy, and Oral Sema 2035E sales

respectively. The corresponding EBIT upsides are +5%, +3%, and +3% respectively.

• In the pipeline, our bull case implies +12% and +10% upside to 2035E sales for CagriSema in obesity and diabetes respectively. The corresponding EBIT upsides are +17% and +14% respectively. But we believe that consensus is still too optimistic on the positioning of CagriSema in both obesity and diabetes (we believe consensus is unwisely discounting mass-market, rather than niche, use). We forecast peak US CagriSema TRx volumes to reach 3% of the market in diabetes and obesity respectively (Exhibit 84 and Exhibit 92).

Our bear scenario:

We expect benign news flow with very few drug catalysts during the next few years (Exhibit 28).

This drug is a fully human monoclonal antibody which targets interleukin 6 (IL-6) to manage cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, and systemic inflammation. (Considering that this drug was launched in the US in 2010 it benefits from many years of safe patient use).

Moreover, at 31/12/25 the company’s net debt/EBITDA was 0.7x which is far below peak financial leverage in EU Pharma (at the bookend Shire geared its balance sheet to 6x in 2016 to facilitate the acquisition of Baxalta). Hence, in our view, theoretically Novo Nordisk has the financial resources to engage in material ‘bridging’ M&A. (Recent radical examples include AstraZeneca’s (O) $3bn rights issue in 2Q19 to fund a 50/50 partnership with Daiichi Sankyo (O; Miki Sogi)) on Enhertu (HER2 breast cancer) and Genmab’s (UP) $9bn acquisition of cancer specialist Merus last year. Both of these transactions involved late stage assets which were/are close to market).

We acknowledge that in 2025 Novo Nordisk spent $11bn to acquire three former Catalent manufacturing plants from the Novo Foundation. But in our view this was a ‘one off’ transaction to alleviate Novo Nordisk’s GLP1 manufacturing constraints. But history suggests that, for pipeline bolstering purposes, Novo tends to adopt a conservative approach to M&A which strongly favors drugs in early to mid-stage clinical development. We hope that we’re wrong, but considering this track record and the composition of Novo’s Board of Directors (Exhibit 30), we’d be surprised if this the company’s strategic approach changes radically going forwards.

US OBESITY MARKET: SOLID VOLUME GROWTH OFFSETTING STICKY DEFLATION

The obesity market is already worth $25bn in 2025. We forecast a 12% value CAGR over the next decade, where the US obesity market will be worth $85bn (Exhibit 42). So far, this story has been dominated by the share battle between Novo and Lilly (O; covered by Courtney Breen). But even with new entrants arriving at the end of the decade, we believe the market will remain a duopoly, with Lilly the dominant player (Exhibit 43).

Our key takeaway from the past five years is simple: in injectables, weight loss wins. Tolerance profile and whether the drug comes with restrictions (e.g. fasting before taking it) are of secondary importance - headline weight loss is the primary needle mover. We think this is largely for two reasons.

1. Obesity drugs are prescribed in primary care settings, where practitioners are relatively time poor. Practitioners will prescribe their “favored” GLP-1 and only move patients onto a different product should their side effects become intolerable.

2. The direct-to-consumer channel in the US has increased the importance of headline weight loss. Patients seeking to lose

weight (reasonably) want the product which maximizes the speed and magnitude of their weight loss. The tolerance profile is a “second order” consideration, whereby a degree of discomfort is tolerated for additional weight loss. (Proven ‘outcomes’ benefits such as stroke reduction are even further down the list).

We think the market dynamics are tough to disrupt. We don’t foresee a share reversal back to 2023 or 2024 levels (where Novo had 60% of the US obesity market). Nor do we think a major disruptor is waiting in the wings. Instead, we think Lilly will remain the dominant player, Novo the (distant) number two, and a tail of players with smaller shares. In our view, both Lilly and Novo should benefit from existing positions on the market, which should be protected by pipeline launches.

US DIABETES MARKET:

SIMILAR DYNAMICS, SIMILAR CONCLUSIONS

Ozempic is Novo’s largest product by global revenue (Exhibit 85) but we forecast yoy revenue declines even prior to the US semaglutide LOE (2032). In our forecasts, the cumulative revenues from (global) sales of Wegovy, Oral Sema, CagriSema (in obesity and diabetes) and all other portfolio and pipeline assets will only just compensate falling Ozempic revenues (Exhibit 86).

The US Most Favored Nation (MFN) pricing and TrumpRx deals included Ozempic and Mounjaro (Lilly’s GLP-1 for diabetes). Thus, we expect contractions in the net price of a similar magnitude to the obesity market (which you re-review here). Ozempic is now available at around $350 per month on TrumpRx and will be available at $250/month via US Medicare (everyone with Type 2 Diabetes (TD2) >65 years of age eligible) and US Medicaid (all households with income below the Federal Poverty Level eligible) from April 2026.

We do not believe that the cash channel will be as important in diabetes as it is in obesity. However, we expect lower cash channel prices (and the lower medicare and medicaid prices) to have spillover effects into the commercial channel. We expect this to result in a lowering of net US diabetes drug prices. In our detailed bottom up US diabetes model, we forecast -30% US price declines in FY26,-15% in 2027, and -10% across 2028-36. Similar to the obesity market, 10-20% yoy price declines are normal in the diabetes segment in the US.

Unlike in Obesity, we don’t model an inflection of GLP-1 prescriptions in 2027, despite the steep price cuts in 2026. In our view, the GLP-1 segment of the US diabetes market is much more mature than obesity (we are approaching a decade of Ozempic use in the US; launched in 2017) and is far less geared towards to the “consumer”. Put simply, the pool of patients actively seeking weight loss is substantially larger than the pool focused solely on tighter glycemic control. Further, we note that many diabetes patients may wish to use Wegovy (or Zepbound) instead of Ozempic (or Mounjaro) in order to benefit from the weight loss as well as reductions in HbA1c levels (the active molecule in Zepbound and Mounjaro is the same; tirzepatide).

CAGRISEMA: IT WON’T BE ENOUGH

In the wider GLP-1 market, we think CagriSema can help stabilize Novo’s share loss in the diabetes market. We understand that Novo will launch CagriSema in diabetes post the launch in obesity (we expect the diabetes launch to be in 2028). REDEFINE-3 (read out in February 2026) demonstrated that patients taking CagriSema achieved greater reductions in Hb1Ac than patients using semaglutide. We’re not aware of any head-to-head data published vs tirzepatide yet (and our understanding is that REDFINE-4 did not have a comparative Hb1Ac reduction as a secondary endpoint), but an indirect (‘meta’) analysis between REDEFINE-3 and SURPASS-1 suggests to us that Mounjaro (tirzepatide) has more effective Hb1Ac reduction (Exhibit 90).

All things considered, we think that CagriSema is better diabetes drug than Ozempic, but Mounjaro will remain the leader. We believe that this is reflected fairly in our 2036 market share forecasts (Exhibit 89). On our estimates, diabetes revenues will comprise 60-70% of “total” CagriSema revenues (taking the sum of both obesity and diabetes) - Exhibit 91.

APPENDIX 1: RARE DISEASE

MASH/EFRUXIFERMIN

In 3Q25, Novo Nordisk confirmed its acquisition of Akero therapeutics for ~$5.2Bn in cash and contingent value rights to gain access to its lead asset efruxifermin. The drug is a bivalent fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21) and beta-klotho receptor analogue used in the treatment of Metabolic-dysfunction Associated Steato-Hepatitis (MASH). MASH is a disease of the liver with a high unmet clinical need, and we believe efruxifermin will be a best-in class drug.

What is MASH?

Metabolic-dysfunction Associated Steato-Hepatitis (MASH), previously known as Non-Alcoholic Steato-Hepatitis (NASH), is a disease process involving the build up of excess fatty tissues within the liver. The fat is deposited through a variety of biological processes, and can irritate healthy liver cells and cause inflammation and scarring to build up.

Scarring in the liver is more broadly known as fibrosis, or cirrhosis in severe cases which can significantly increase the risk of liver cancer. MASH can cause fibrosis, and although the two are strongly linked are strictly speaking separate. Hence, one can exist without the other.

In the US alone, approximately 25% of the population will develop metabolic-dysfunction associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), a milder version of MASH in their lifetime, with 20-25% of those patients progressing to MASH (Exhibit 95). The prevalence of MASH in the US is predicted to increase 63% from 16.5 million cases in 2015 to 27.0 million cases in 2030, with similar rates across Europe.

MASH can often be silent with no symptoms, hence the discrepancy between the prevalence of the disease and the number of patients actually diagnosed and treated.

Those who do have symptoms suffer with tiredness, general weakness, some discomfort in the tummy and occasionally some itching and bruising. Although by itself it is rarely a debilitating disease, MASH is a stepping stone to more severe scarring leading to cancer and death. As such, it is considered a serious health problem, and the second leading driver of liver transplantation worldwide.

Disease burden and treatment paradigm

Given MASH often forms part of a constellation of health problems, estimating its economic impact can be difficult. Nevertheless, conservative estimates place the medical costs of MASH at $78.6Bn by 2040 in the US alone, whilst MASH associated productivity loss will be close to $246Bn during same timescale. Per patient medical costs in the US are expected to be $3,601 by 2040, whilst individual patient productivity loss annually is expected to top $11,300 in the same year. Epidemiological research by Madrigal Therapeutics (NR; a leader in MASH), demonstrates that in addition to a growing MASH patient population, the percentage of patients being treated will also grow substantially in the medium to long term as awareness of the disease increases.

To diagnose MASH, physicians may use bloodwork to examine liver function tests, ultrasound scans, and (rarely) a biopsy (removal of a sample via surgery) of the liver are conducted to confirm the disease pathology. With progress in ultrasound technology and protocols, the need for biopsy has reduced significantly and clinicians are growing increasingly confident in treating the disease in the absence of a confirmed tissue sample.

In terms of treatment, a healthy diet and physical activity is often the first line to minimize circulating fats in the bloodstream, and mitigate the metabolic risk factors contributing to MASH.

Bariatric surgery (forced stomach shrinkage by decreasing the diameter of the gastric tract) to treat obesity is evidenced to be up to 90% effective in treating MASH. Meanwhile, liver transplantation can be offered in cases of MASH where liver function is most severely restricted.

Medical (i.e. drug) treatment usually starts with cholesterol lowering medication and good diabetic control. Although medical treatment specific to MASH is uncommon with only a few approved agents licensed for the disease.

Current therapeutic landscape

Rezdiffra (Madrigal therapeutics; unrated) is a drug that mimics the thyroid hormone to promote fat breakdown and dissolution. More specifically, Rezdiffra binds to the thyroid hormone receptor beta (THR-beta) in liver fat to promote its breakdown.

In the phase III MAESTRO-NASH trial program, Rezdiffra was compared to placebo to assess fibrosis and MASH on ultrasound imaging. 25.9% of patients achieved at least a 1 stage improvement in their F score without worsening of MASH compared to 14.2% in the placebo group (p<0.001). No concerning safety signals were reported and the drug was approved as the first ever licensed treatment exclusively for MASH in 1Q2024. Following sales of $180m in 2024, consensus (Bloomberg) projects Rezdiffra peak sales of $5Bn in 2031 for a CAGR (25-31e) of 61%.

The second, and only other medication with an indication in MASH is semaglutide, known as Wegovy (Novo Nordisk; O), an analogue of the glucagon like peptide 1 (GLP-1) receptor. This drug acts on the hormone pathway that promotes appetite suppression, lowers blood sugar levels and improves control of body weight. Originally indicated for tackling obesity, the phase III ESSENCE study trialed Wegovy against placebo in MASH patients, evaluating whether there was improvement in liver stiffness and fibrosis reflected on ultrasound imaging and bloodwork.

Spotlight on efruxifermin

Efruxifermin is a member of a new class of MASH medications that mimic the activity of fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21 . FGF21 is a naturally occurring hormone that is often described as a “master metabolic regulator” (Exhibit 99) which controls and reduces fat accumulation throughout the body and thus comprises healthy metabolism. This drug is administered by subcutaneous (under the skin) injection on a weekly basis.

By providing an analogue that is longer acting than the hormone present in the body, efruxifermin prolongs and amplifies the positive effects of normally present FGF21. Some of these desirable effects include healing of the scar tissue in the liver, and slowing down the MASH disease process.

With MASH induced fibrosis in particular, FGF21 has demonstrated impressive potential in vitro (in a test tube). As such, we believe this platform is of particular interest in next generation MASH therapy, and we believe that it could compete within the sparsely populated treatment landscape for MASH.

In the phase IIb HARMONY study, efruxifermin reduced fibrosis by >1 F stage in at least 75% of patients compared to 24% in placebo (p<0.001). No concerning safety signals were reported and the adverse event profile was favorable in our view.

This primary endpoint in HARMONY was measured on liver histology at 96 weeks and given the statistically significant positive results, efruxifermin was in our view a clinically meaningful treatment that will affect the standard of care. Efruxifermin is currently in three different phase III trials (SYNCHRONY program), evaluating the same endpoints as in phase II but in more severe MASH patients and those with cirrhosis already established. The first phase 3 trial in this program is expected to read out in 1H26.

APPENDIX 2: A GENERALIST’S GUIDE TO GLP-1S: WITH A ZOOM IN ON SEMAGLUTIDE

The Main Mechanisms of Action

Obesity drugs, in general, target our “hunger-related” hormones. As the body’s digestive system (and it’s links with the brain) are extremely complicated, there are numerous hormones needed for the body to function. A drug (such as semaglutide; active ingredient in Wegovy) may only target a single hormone (and is termed a “single agonist”) others (such as tirzepatide, active ingredient in Zepbound) target two hormones (“double agonist”). Below we list the main mechanisms of actions:

• GLP-1 receptor agonist. Mimics the Glucagon-Like Peptide 1 (a hormone) by inhibiting the release of glucagon (hormone raising blood sugar levels) and stimulating the production of insulin (a hormone that lowers blood sugar levels) thus reducing hyperglycemia which leads to lower blood sugar levels. Furthermore, GLP-1s reduce hunger and slow the GI (gastro intestinal) food flow leading to a decrease in appetite.

• Amylin receptor agonist. Mimics amylin (hormone secreted with insulin) which inhibits glucagon secretion and affects

gastric emptying plus food intake.

• GIP receptor agonist. Mimics the Glucose-dependant Insulinotropic Peptide (hormone) which stimulates insulin production and sensitivity leading to a better insulin penetration in the bloodstream, thus lowering blood sugar levels. The hormone also reduces stomach acid production, leading to a slow GI food flow resulting in a decrease in appetite.

• Glucagon receptor agonist. Mimics glucagon which increases blood glucose levels and breaks down lipids and amino

acids. Exogenous intake of glucagon leads to increased energy expenditure with a lower food intake.

• CB1 receptor inverse agonist. The peripheral cannabinoid receptor 1 modulates appetite and energy homeostasis but also

metabolic inflammation induced by insulin resistance.

Side Effects and Tolerance

The most common side effects from GLP-1s are gastro-intestinal (GI) problems. The primary mechanism of action of GLP-1s is to alter the way our “hunger hormones” behave and interact with each other. It is, therefore, unsurprising that gut- related issues are the primary side effect from taking GLP-1s.

• Nausea, vomiting, constipation, bloating, and digestive reflux are all common GI adverse events. Especially towards the start of treatment, where the body’s digestive process is undergoing a “re-wiring”, side effects may manifest themselves. For most people, the side effects are manageable (or tolerated for the benefit of the weight loss).

• Fatigue, headaches, dizziness, and taste changes can also be caused by GLP-1 usage. As the body readjusts to a new level of caloric intake, the body undergoes a “metabolic stress test”. These side effects can be prominent when people are adjusting to new dosing levels, and can exacerbated by dehydration (GLP-1s also suppress the “thirst hormones”).

Whilst we think the tolerance profile of a drug is important, the main driver behind the success of an anti-obesity product remains headline the weight loss. Patients are willing to accept a worse tolerance profile in order to achieve larger amounts of weight loss. Most drugs (including those in the pipeline) have a similar discontinuation due to adverse events rates - certainly not different enough to shape patient behavior. We think the weight loss-tolerance trade off will become more important as more drugs enter the market, but weight-loss will remain the driving force.

Semaglutide: A Deep(er) Dive

Semaglutide is a long-acting glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonist (GLP-1 RA). Glucagon-like peptide-1 is a naturally occurring hormone that helps the body regulate blood sugar (glucose) levels, appetite, and the speed at which nutrients are moved from the stomach into the bloodstream.

Semaglutide is a modified version of the naturally occurring hormone. The GLP-1 drugs increase the length of time it lasts in the body and activates the GLP-1 receptors (starting signals) more steadily. Semaglutide has three primary effects on the body:

1. Pancreas: Increases the amount of insulin secreted when glucose is high (normally just after eating) but not when glucose

levels are low (lowering the risk of hypoglycemic - crashes in blood sugar levels - attacks).

2. Stomach: Slows gastric emptying (slows the rate that nutrients enter the bloodstream) which “smooths” out the body’s

glucose response. This lowers strain on the pancreas (reducing the risk of developing diabetes in the first instance, as well as slowing the pancreatic strain in diabetes patients) and helps patients feel “fuller” for longer.
